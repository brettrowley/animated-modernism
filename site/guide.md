# The Rulebook

A visitor's edition of the rules behind these animations.

Every painting here was traced and animated by Claude, then directed by Brett Rowley. Each time something felt wrong, the note became a rule, and the next painting started from a better Rulebook. Over eight paintings and eleven versions, 43 corrections were distilled into the 29 settled rules below.

The early paintings needed a lot of direction (A II alone took 13 notes). By the end, two paintings in a row needed none at all.

The rules fall into four parts: how a painting is read and traced, the motions available, how a single shape moves, and how a whole piece is composed. The last section tells the story painting by painting.

## 1. Seeing the painting

Before anything moves, the painting is rebuilt as clean geometry and read as a composition.

#### 1.1 Idealise the geometry onto the painting's grid
Not a pixel trace. Find the rectangles, lines and circles, then fit them to the grid underneath, so straight sides are perfectly straight and shapes that look parallel really are. Some paintings are built from circles instead, sharing radii and meeting at exact points.

#### 1.2 Let shapes run past the canvas where the painting implies it
A shape cut off by the frame keeps going beyond it, so it can travel in from outside.

#### 1.3 Identify shadows
A stripe that tracks a shape and only darkens what it crosses is that shape's shadow. It has no motion of its own; it moves exactly with its caster. Reflections work the same way.

#### 1.4 Match the spirit, not the exact colours
Paint doesn't follow digital colour rules. Aim for the character of each overlap.

#### 1.5 Model each shape as tinted glass, not reduced opacity
Each shape filters what's behind it and adds a little light of its own, so overlaps mix the way coloured glass does. Opaque paint is glass that lets nothing through.

#### 1.6 Draw order is blend order
Shapes are stacked bottom to top, and the order changes the colours. When a painting asks for an impossible order, the larger overlaps win.

#### 1.7 Where each overlap has its own colour, draw it as one shape clipped by another
Some painters choose each overlap's colour rather than mixing it. Then every overlap is drawn as its own shape, only where its shapes meet, and it follows them as they move.

### Reading the composition

Before choosing any motion, five questions:

1. **What are the shapes?** As they look inside the frame: long bars, near-squares, curves, circles, lines.
2. **Which belong together?** Shapes that share a direction, a region or an idea form a family. Full circles stand apart; very heavy shapes stand alone.
3. **What sets the stage?** Shapes that run past the frame come first. Failing that, the family the others are built on.
4. **Where is the room?** A motion needs space ahead of it to be seen.
5. **What is the main event?** The heaviest shapes and biggest gestures belong in the crescendo.

Every motion has a physical story, and every shape's kind suggests one. This is the full vocabulary, built up painting by painting.

## 3. How a shape moves

Rules for a single motion, from its first frame to the moment it settles.

#### 3.1 Move along the painting's own lines
Shapes travel along their own edges, unfold from slivers or from nothing, and turn about pivots the composition implies.

#### 3.2 Give every motion a physical story
Falling, braking, stretching, snapping. Never start from an easing preset.

#### 3.3 Let geometry set the numbers
Distances, pivots and speeds come from the shapes and the canvas.

#### 3.4 Whatever is added must belong to the painting's geometry
Anything temporary, like the head that draws a line, speaks the painting's own language: a pen for a painted line, a gouge's point for a woodcut.

#### 3.5 Curved areas sweep radially; curved lines are drawn
A curved area is swept out by a line turning about a pivot, never swollen into shape. A curved line is drawn along its length, like any line.

#### 3.6 Full circles travel in, changing size
A circle arrives on a curve, growing like something approaching or shrinking like something moving away. Inside a ring, circles spiral in like a vortex.

#### 3.7 Nothing pops into view
Everything emerges: from off the canvas, from a sliver, from nothing, or from another shape. What's hidden at rest stays hidden in every frame along the way.

#### 3.8 Don't hold a shape far thinner than everything around it
A long thin sliver reads as a stray line. Better to open the shape from nothing.

#### 3.9 A shape's first appearance must show its true extent
If another shape hides part of it as it starts, what opens no longer matches what appeared.

#### 3.10 Arrivals only slow down
A shape brakes into its resting place and never speeds up once slowing. Only an impact arrives fast.

#### 3.11 A motion needs room to be seen
Measure the space ahead first. A shape close to the edge suits a motion that starts from itself, like a fold.

#### 3.12 Choose an entrance by where the shape's ends are
A shape that runs past the frame at both ends lands like a beam. One with room ahead can pass through and break away instead.

#### 3.13 A bounce needs speed at the moment of arrival
A bounce is what happens when something fast hits a stop. Never as decoration: a shape that eases into place has nothing to rebound with.

#### 3.14 Bounce along the major axis
On the short axis, a bounce reads as a wobble.

## 4. Composing the whole piece

Rules for how the shapes share a few seconds.

#### 4.1 Group shapes by family, separate them in time
A family shares one motion and one ending, but each member gets its own moment.

#### 4.2 A shape with a different role gets a different motion
Never reuse a motion just because it worked on a similar shape.

#### 4.3 Build from the fundamental family
If one family feels like the painting's construction, it arrives first and the rest build on it.

#### 4.4 Use direction for visual balance
Two shapes moving at once go opposite ways; three or more moving together read as one gesture. Different families never move the same way at the same time.

#### 4.5 Shape the whole animation as a bell curve of motion
One or two shapes to catch the eye, a crescendo with motion across the canvas, then one or two to guide the eye out.

#### 4.6 Open with what sets the stage
Shapes that run past the frame come first, a subtle one easing the eye in. Failing that, the family that defines the grid, or the shape everything else rests on.

#### 4.7 No dead tails
The ending is never one shape quietly finishing while everything else waits.

#### 4.8 An intro runs about 3–4 seconds, once
Short, and played once, holding on the finished painting. More shapes means more overlap, not more time, though the busiest painting earned six seconds.

## 5. The paintings

What each painting taught.

### A18 · László Moholy-Nagy, 1927
The exploratory first piece, made before there was a Rulebook. Its notes became the first draft: arrivals that brake, nothing popping into view, shapes that travel along the painting's own lines.

### Eccentric Construction · László Moholy-Nagy, 1921
The first painting made from the Rulebook, and 11 corrections. It added families, room to be seen, and the bell curve of motion.

### E IV · László Moholy-Nagy, 1922
Nine corrections: curved shapes sweep rather than swell, shadows move with their casters, and heavy shapes belong in the crescendo.

### A II · László Moholy-Nagy, 1924
The most corrected painting, with 13 notes, on symmetry, partners, circles and quiet shapes.

### Suprematism · Kazimir Malevich, 1916
The first busy painting, 48 shapes. Seven corrections settled how long a piece should run and how it should end.

### Moon Bridge · Herbert Bayer, 1976
No corrections. Its flat, chosen colours led to drawing every overlap as its own shape, and its crescents to the hoist and the lune.

### Multiplex A · Josef Albers, 1947
A woodcut, and no corrections. It taught the Rulebook about lines: how they're cut, how they join, and which tool draws them.

### Circles in a Circle · Wassily Kandinsky, 1923
The busiest piece, 57 elements, and three corrections: the circles spiral into the ring, the smallest simply grow, and it runs six seconds.
